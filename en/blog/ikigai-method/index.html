The ikigai method: what it is and how to apply it

What almost everyone calls “the ikigai method” is a four-circle exercise. It helps you get organised, but it isn’t what it promises. Here’s how to use it well and what it lacks.

Manel Rueda Author and creator of IKIGAIER 21 September 2026

When someone searches for “the ikigai method” they’re almost always looking for the same thing: the four-circle exercise that promises to find your purpose by crossing what you love, what you’re good at, what the world needs and what you’re paid for. We’ll use it, but with our eyes open: it isn’t a Japanese method and it doesn’t do what the poster says.

What “the ikigai method” is

The so-called ikigai method is, in practice, a reflection template. You fill in four areas and see where they overlap. At the centre, in theory, would be your ikigai. As a tool to clear your head when you’re lost in your work or vocation, it works: it makes you look at once at things we usually look at separately.

Now the small print: that framework isn’t Japanese in origin. It came from a diagram about purpose and was labelled “ikigai” years later. So calling it “the ikigai method” is, honestly, a bit of a cheat. It’s a good method for professional self-knowledge; it isn’t the definition of ikigai.

How to apply it well

If you’re going to do the exercise, do it without the pressure of finding the magic box in the middle. Take it as four questions to think about your life, not an equation to solve:

  • What you love. What you do that absorbs you until you lose track of time.
  • What you’re good at. What you’re genuinely good at, even if you don’t think much of it.
  • What the world needs. Where what you do serves someone else.
  • What you’re paid for. How you support yourself —honestly accepting that this is real, but not the measure of meaning.

The key is not demanding that all four coincide. If they fit, good. If not, it doesn’t mean you have no ikigai. The perfect intersection —the famous ikigai zone— is tiny and, for many people, empty. Japanese ikigai doesn’t live there: it can be in a single circle, or outside all four.

The four-circle method describes what you do well. It forgets the most important thing: where you do it from.

What the popular method lacks

This is where IKIGAIER proposes something else —and it’s fair to say it’s our own elaboration, not Japanese tradition. The four-circle method stays entirely in one dimension: doing. What you produce, build, contribute. We call it Active Ikigai.

But it leaves out the other half: being. Where you do what you do from, with what presence and what coherence, without betraying yourself in the small decisions. We call it Conscious Ikigai. You can have a flawless life across the four circles and, at the same time, be quite far from yourself.

The IKIGAIER method, in short

Rather than filling in a diagram, the IKIGAIER method is practised as a journey of five gestures, designed to bring doing and being together:

  • Stop and listen.
  • Look at where you’ve drifted from yourself.
  • Recognise the masks that hold you up.
  • Come down from the mind into the body.
  • Close and choose a first step.

It isn’t more complex than the diagram; it’s more honest about what a method can and can’t do. None of them “gives” you your ikigai: they help you recognise the one that’s already there.

The method can be practised alone, but sometimes you need to live it with others.
