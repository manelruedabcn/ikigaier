Musubi and ikigai: the knot between what you do and how you live it

Musubi is a Japanese word that means to tie and, at the same time, names the force that brings things into being. It is not part of ikigai, but it helps with something the four circles never explain: what happens between what you do and the way you live it, and between you and the people who turn up in your life.

Manel Rueda Author and creator of ikigaier 22 November 2026

When people talk about ikigai, they nearly always talk about pieces: what you love, what you are good at, what the world needs, what you can be paid for. Far less is said about how the pieces come together. Someone can have every one of them and live them apart, like the drawers of a cabinet opened one at a time. To think about that joining, I have found help in a Japanese word that rarely appears in books about ikigai: musubi.

What musubi means

Musubi is written in two ways, and both matter. The everyday one is 結び, from the verb musubu, to tie. It is the word for the knot in a lace, a bow, a promise that is sealed. The other is older and belongs to the myths, written 産霊 or 産巣日 and sometimes read musuhi. According to Kokugakuin University’s Encyclopedia of Shinto, musu carries the sense of creation and growth, and hi that of an unseen, mysterious working. Together they name whatever causes heaven, earth and all things to come into being.

In Shinto, musubi is the power of becoming. In the creation story of the Kojiki, compiled in 712, two of the first three deities to appear carry the word in their names: Takamimusubi and Kamimusubi. The same university sums it up as the spirit of birth and becoming, the forces that create and harmonise.

What interests me is that the language does not separate the two senses. Tying and bringing to life share one word, as if old Japanese had understood that whatever is created comes from something being joined.

From the gods to a rice ball

Anyone who has been into a Japanese shop knows the omusubi, rice pressed by hand, often into a triangle and wrapped in seaweed. It is also called onigiri, from nigiru, to squeeze. Musubi as a name for shaped rice spread during the Edo period, between 1603 and 1868, and the polite o was added later. Some link it to the gods of the Kojiki and some dispute that. What is certain is that the word carries both meanings at once.

A recent story shows this well. After the Kobe earthquake in January 1995, volunteers fed thousands of displaced people mainly with rice balls, because they can be made in quantity from very little, carried in the hand and eaten without plates. In 2000, an association devoted to Japanese food named 17 January, the day of the earthquake, Omusubi Day. It chose that word rather than onigiri precisely for what it evokes: the bonds between people.

What it has to do with ikigai

In Japanese, ikigai is an ordinary word for whatever makes life worth living. I go into it slowly in what ikigai means. Musubi is another word from another tradition, and nobody in Japan will tell you the two belong together. Connecting them is my own reading, and I offer it as such.

In the ikigaier practice I speak of two faces of ikigai. One is active ikigai: what you do and contribute, what the four circles draw. The other is conscious ikigai: the way you live what you do, whether you are present, whether you betray yourself in small decisions. I explain it more fully in what is ikigaier.

Musubi gives me an image for what happens between the two. Two loose cords hold nothing, however good they are. Once knotted, they bear weight, and something appears that was not there before. Ikigai works in a similar way. You can have a job that fits the diagram and do it every day with your mind elsewhere. And you can live with great attention and do nothing with it. In both cases the knot is missing. What I call ikigai, in its full sense, is what is born when the two are tied.

That changes a question. Instead of “what am I missing?”, which usually sends you looking for a new piece, a less comfortable one appears: “what do I already have that I have not tied?” Very often the pieces are there. What is missing is the joining.

The people who turn up

Japanese has an expression that joins musubi to another word, en (縁), with Buddhist roots: the thread linking a cause to what eventually happens. En-musubi is the tying of those bonds. At shrines people mostly pray for it to find a partner, but Izumo, the best-known of them, understands it more broadly: the ties with family, with work and with the people around you.

I have a theory that I cannot prove and that has nonetheless stayed with me all my life. I believe we are more connected than it seems, and that when you have something to do or to enjoy with someone, that person turns up. I don’t call it chance. In Spanish I play with two words that differ by a single letter: not casualidad, chance, but causalidad, causality. En-musubi is the Japanese way of saying something similar, and I like that it has a name.

If I bring this back to ikigai, the knot is not only tied between what you do and how you live it. It is also tied with others. Some things only make sense once the person to do them with appears.

A small knot for this week

A knot isn’t tied by thinking about knots. It is tied with your hands, around something concrete. If you would like to try:

  • Choose one thing you do every day out of duty or habit: a journey, a meeting, making a meal.
  • Before you start, ask yourself who it is for or what it holds up. One sentence is enough.
  • Do it this once knowing the answer, without changing anything else.

The task stays the same. What changes is that you are tying it to something. If at some point you notice a difference between doing it like this and doing it on autopilot, you have seen a small musubi. If you don’t notice one, that tells you something too.

The idea of the two faces of ikigai, and why most methods keep only one, is developed in El ikigai que no te contaron and in the ikigai method and the half it leaves out.

Frequently asked questions

What does musubi mean?

Musubi is a Japanese word that joins two ideas: to tie or knot, and the force that makes things come into being and grow. In Shinto it names the power of creation, and in daily life it is used for knots, bonds between people and rice balls.

What is en-musubi?

It is the tying of the bonds that connect people. It joins musubi to en, a word with Buddhist roots for the thread linking a cause to what eventually happens. It is mostly associated with finding a partner, but it also covers family, work and community.

Why are rice balls called omusubi?

Musubi as a name for hand-pressed rice spread during the Edo period, and the polite o was added later. Today it sits alongside onigiri. Whether the name is directly linked to the myth is debated, but both senses, tying and creating, live in the word.

Is musubi part of ikigai?

Not in Japanese tradition: they are different words. Connecting them is a reading of the ikigaier practice, which uses musubi as an image of the knot between active ikigai, what you do, and conscious ikigai, how you live it.
