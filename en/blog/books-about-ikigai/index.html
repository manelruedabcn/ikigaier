Books about ikigai: which to read (and in what order)

Not all books about ikigai say the same thing, and some don’t even describe Japanese ikigai. An honest guide depending on what you’re looking for.

Manel Rueda Author and creator of IKIGAIER 21 September 2026

Search for “books about ikigai” and you’ll mostly find one, the bestseller, plus a cloud of imitations. They don’t all say the same thing, and it’s worth knowing what you’re getting before you buy. Here are the ones truly worth it, ordered by what you’re looking for —with a note on where the Japanese concept ends and the Western interpretation begins.

Transparency note: one of the books on this list is mine. I flag it clearly and tell you exactly what it’s useful for and what it isn’t, just as with the others.

The one everyone knows

Ikigai: The Japanese Secret to a Long and Happy Life — Héctor García and Francesc Miralles (2016)

The bestseller that took the word around the world. Light, pleasant and full of stories from Okinawa. If it’s your first contact, it works as a way in. Its limit: it mixes ikigai with longevity and happiness —a more Western than Japanese association— and helped popularise the diagram. Read it knowing it’s a friendly introduction, not the last word.

If you want the real Japanese ikigai

Ikigai ni tsuite (“On ikigai”) — Mieko Kamiya (1966)

The founding work. Psychiatrist Mieko Kamiya studied what makes a life worth living through her work with patients. It’s the text all serious writing on ikigai starts from. Hard to find in translation, but it’s the root: if you want to know what ikigai was before the West reinvented it, start here (or with what has been written about her).

The Little Book of Ikigai — Ken Mogi (2017)

Neuroscientist Ken Mogi takes ikigai back to Japan and away from the diagram. He talks about five pillars and everyday life. More faithful to the original concept than the bestseller, and still accessible. A good second read.

If you want rigour

What Makes Life Worth Living? — Gordon Matthews (1996)

A serious anthropological study of how Japanese and Americans make sense of their lives. It isn’t a self-help book; it’s for anyone who wants to understand ikigai in its real cultural context, without formulas.

If it’s the diagram that bothers you

El Ikigai que no te contaron — Manel Rueda

The one I wrote (in Spanish). It comes precisely from the suspicion that the ikigai we were sold —the four-circle one— falls short. It doesn’t repeat the diagram: it proposes distinguishing Active Ikigai (what you do) from Conscious Ikigai (where you do it from), and looks honestly at the distance between what we do and who we are. For anyone who already read the bestseller and was left feeling something was missing.

And if you want to move from reading to doing, the workbook Camina sin separarte de ti turns that perspective into questions and exercises to write by hand.

In what order to read them

  • First contact: García and Miralles, or go straight to this guide on what ikigai is, which spares you the usual misunderstandings.
  • For the real concept: Ken Mogi and, if you dare, Kamiya.
  • To go beyond the diagram: mine, and then the workbook to put it into practice.

No book will hand you your ikigai —that is recognised by walking it— but the good ones save you years of looking in the wrong place.
