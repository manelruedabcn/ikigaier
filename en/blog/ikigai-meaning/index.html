Ikigai: what the word really means

Before it became a four-circle diagram, ikigai was —and still is— an ordinary word in Japanese. It’s worth looking at what it really says.

Manel Rueda Author and creator of IKIGAIER 21 September 2026

“Ikigai means reason for being.” That’s the definition you’ll find almost everywhere, and it isn’t wrong. The problem is that it’s a shop-window translation: it sounds deep, fits in a headline and leaves out almost everything interesting. If you came here looking for what the word means exactly, let’s take a little more care.

How ikigai is written in Japanese

In Japanese, ikigai is usually written 生き甲斐, and also in a softer form mixing scripts, 生きがい. It is pronounced ee-kee-guy, with three clean syllables.

The word has two pieces:

  • iki (生き): to do with living, with life, with being alive.
  • gai (甲斐): points to worth, merit, effect; the idea that something is worthwhile or bears fruit. It comes from an old word, kai, which expresses exactly that: that an effort has a result that justifies it.

Together, ikigai is closer to “what makes life worth living” or “what gives living its value” than to an abstract “reason for being”. The difference isn’t cosmetic: a reason for being sounds like a solemn declaration; ikigai is the concrete texture of why it’s worth being here.

The comfortable translation and what it leaves out

“Reason for being” compresses the word until it sounds grandiose. It suggests we’re talking about one great justification for your existence, something you either find or lack. And the Japanese term doesn’t work like that.

Ikigai isn’t the answer to “what do I exist for?”. It’s the answer, far more modest, to “why is this day worth it?”.

That’s why the most careful translators like to circle the word rather than close it: “what gives meaning to your days”, “what makes life worth living”, “the reasons to get up in the morning”. They all say something true and none exhausts it. Ikigai is partly untranslatable: not because it’s mystical, but because it names an everyday experience for which in English we use whole sentences.

Not one, but many (and often small)

Here is the nuance most often lost. In Japanese you can have several ikigai at once, and they tend to be human-sized: a child, a craft done with care, the vegetable garden, a friend, the morning routine. They don’t need to be spectacular or profitable. An ikigai may not earn money, nobody else may see it, and it’s still an ikigai.

This clashes head-on with the Western version of the word, where ikigai is presented as a single meeting point —what you love, what you’re good at, what the world needs and what you’re paid for— that you have to find. That image, the famous diagram, isn’t Japanese and doesn’t describe the original meaning; I go into it in detail in the guide on what ikigai is and what nobody told you.

Why the meaning changes how you look for it

It might seem like a debate about nuances, but it isn’t. What you believe the word means decides how you look for it.

If ikigai is “the reason for my life”, it’s easy to end up waiting for a revelation, to feel you’re missing something important and to see life as an exam you haven’t passed. If ikigai is “what makes today worthwhile”, the task changes: it’s no longer finding one huge, unique thing, but recognising the reasons that are probably already there and paying them more attention.

It’s a weighty difference. The first version leaves you permanently in debt. The second sends you back to look at your own life, which is where ikigai has always been.

In one sentence

Ikigai (生き甲斐) means, as honestly as possible, what makes life worth living: not one great single mission, but the reasons —often small and many— why it’s worth being here. “Reason for being” is a good cover label; the content is more everyday and more yours.
