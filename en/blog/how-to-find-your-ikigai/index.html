How to find your ikigai

You don’t find it by filling in a diagram or waiting for a revelation. You recognise it by looking at your own life more honestly than usual.

Manel Rueda Author and creator of IKIGAIER 21 September 2026

“How do I find my ikigai?” is one of the most searched questions, and it’s almost always answered with the same four-circle diagram, as if it were a form. You fill in the boxes, cross the areas and there’s your purpose. If only it were that mechanical.

Ikigai isn’t a hidden object you find, but something you recognise: it’s almost always already there, in your life, hidden among everyday things. So the useful question isn’t “where do I look for it?” but “how do I learn to see it?”. That’s what this is about.

Put another way: rather than discovering your ikigai the way you’d discover a treasure, it’s about learning to see what already holds you up. It isn’t a matter of knowing how to do it with a technique, but of no longer looking the other way.

Start by observing, not deciding

The first mistake is treating it as a decision: sitting down to choose your purpose once and for all. The pressure to decide blocks you. Instead, for a few days, become an observer of yourself. Don’t change anything; just look. What you do, when time slips by unnoticed, what leaves you with more energy and what drains it.

Ikigai isn’t decided in an afternoon. It’s recognised by paying attention to what already holds you up.

Questions to start looking

You don’t need to answer them all at once or in writing, though writing helps. Let them work for a few days:

Questions to walk with

  • What do you do that absorbs you until you lose track of time?
  • What would you keep doing even if nobody saw it or thanked you for it?
  • In what small moment of the day do you feel you’re where you should be?
  • What were you asked to do “less” of as a child because you got too excited about it?
  • Where have you drifted away from yourself to meet what was expected?

Signs to look for

Ikigai rarely announces itself with fireworks. It shows in details that are easy to overlook:

  • Absorption. Activities in which time disappears. It’s one of the most reliable clues.
  • The energy afterwards. Not what excites you beforehand, but how you feel after doing it: more whole or more empty.
  • What you’d do for free. What you already do without anyone paying you or watching.
  • The small and repeated. Meaning often lies in an everyday gesture, not in a big project.

Two mistakes that drag out the search

The first is looking for something big and dismissing the small. Many people discard their ikigai precisely because it’s modest: “this can’t be it, it’s too ordinary”. The second is confusing productivity with meaning: filling your diary doesn’t bring you closer to ikigai, sometimes it covers it up. Doing a lot isn’t the same as doing something worthwhile.

And there’s a third, deeper one: looking for the ikigai others expect of you. Sometimes what we call “not finding it” is really not daring to recognise the one we already have, because it doesn’t fit what we’re supposed to want.

A concrete first step

If you want to start today, you don’t need a retreat or great clarity. Pick one of the questions above and live with it for a week: watch yourself in its light, without forcing an answer.
