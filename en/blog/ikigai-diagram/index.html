The ikigai diagram: what it is and where it really came from

The four intersecting circles are the most famous image of ikigai. They are also the most misleading: they’re not Japanese and they don’t describe the original concept.

Manel Rueda Author and creator of IKIGAIER 21 September 2026

Search for “ikigai” once and you’ll run into the diagram: four circles —what you love, what you are good at, what the world needs, what you can be paid for— and, at the centre, your ikigai. It’s such a clear and repeated image that hardly anyone asks where it comes from. And where it comes from changes everything.

What the diagram shows

The idea is simple: your ikigai would sit at the point where all four things intersect. What you love and are good at would be your passion; what you love and the world needs, your mission; what the world needs and you’re paid for, your vocation; what you’re good at and paid for, your profession. And at the centre, the perfect balance of all four.

As a framework to get your thoughts in order when you feel lost at work, it’s not bad. The problem starts when it’s presented as ancient Japanese wisdom. Because it isn’t.

The “ikigai zone”: what that centre is

That central point where the four circles meet is often called the ikigai zone: the place where what you love, what you’re good at, what the world needs and what you’re paid for all coincide. It’s presented as perfect balance, the goal to reach.

It’s worth looking at that zone calmly, because it hides the diagram’s trap. Drawn out, the intersection of the four is tiny —and in real life, for many people, simply empty. Presenting that minuscule box as the condition for meaning leaves almost everyone feeling something is missing. Japanese ikigai doesn’t live in any zone of perfect fit: it can be in a single circle, or in none of the four. The “ikigai zone” is a goal the West drew, not the place where meaning is really found.

Where it really came from

The diagram has a date and authors, and none of them is Japanese. In 2011, Spanish author Andrés Zuzunaga published a four-circle diagram about purpose —not ikigai— crossing what you love, what you have a talent for, what the world needs and what you can be paid for.

In 2014, British blogger Marc Winn took that same framework, replaced the word “purpose” with “ikigai” —inspired by a Dan Buettner TED talk on longevity in Okinawa— and published it on his blog. That crossover, a Western diagram about purpose with a Japanese word, went viral and became the image the whole world now associates with ikigai.

Worth remembering

The diagram was born talking about purpose and was labelled ikigai three years later. It is a Western construction on top of a Japanese word, not the Japanese word.

Why it doesn’t represent Japanese ikigai

Ikigai as understood and studied in Japan —from psychiatrist Mieko Kamiya in 1966 to today— is far less spectacular than the diagram’s. It isn’t a point of perfect balance between four variables. It’s the reasons, often small and many, that make life worth living for someone: a craft done with care, a bond, a routine.

The biggest difference lies in one box: “what you’re paid for”. The diagram demands that your ikigai be profitable too. Japanese ikigai doesn’t ask that at all. An ikigai may not earn money, the world may never notice it, and it’s still an ikigai.

The diagram tells you that you don’t have an ikigai until the four things fit. Real ikigai demands none of that.

What it is and isn’t useful for

Not being Japanese doesn’t make it useless. As a clarity exercise, the diagram helps: it makes you look at once at what you like, what you’re good at, what you contribute and how you support yourself financially. For someone stuck in their career, it’s a good blank page.

  • It works for sorting out ideas about your work and seeing which pieces you have and which are missing.
  • It doesn’t work as a definition of ikigai, or as an exam you pass or fail depending on how many circles you cross.
  • It misleads when it suggests that without all four —money included— no meaning is possible.

Use it for what it is: a Western tool for professional self-knowledge, not a map of the soul. And if you want the full picture —what ikigai is, what the word means and what the West added— you’ll find it in the guide on what ikigai is.
