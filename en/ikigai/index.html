Ikigai: what it really is and what nobody told you

Almost everything circulating about ikigai —starting with the famous four-circle diagram— isn’t Japanese. It’s worth separating what we know about the original concept, what the West added later and what you can do with all of it.

Few words have travelled so far and arrived so changed as ikigai. In barely a decade it went from an everyday Japanese term to a motivational poster: four intersecting circles and, at the centre, the promise of finding the meaning of your life.

That version is comfortable, tidy and, to a large extent, invented outside Japan. It isn’t that it’s a lie: it’s told as if it were an ancient tradition when in fact it’s only a few years old and has a fairly traceable origin. And when something important is told badly, it’s worth telling it again, properly.

This page is that retelling. We’ll separate three things that almost always get mixed up: what we know about Japanese ikigai, what the West added later and what you can do with all of it today. Not to dwell on the historical anecdote, but because whether the word is any use to you or just gives you a pretty picture depends on that distinction.

What the word ikigai means

Ikigai is written in Japanese as 生き甲斐. It is made up of iki (生き), to do with living or life, and gai (甲斐), which points to worth, merit, what is worthwhile. Together they’re usually translated as “reason to live” or, more precisely and less epically, “what makes life worth living”.

That nuance deserves a pause. It doesn’t say “the purpose of your life”, singular and capitalised. It says, rather, what makes it worth getting up today. It’s a difference in size and tone: less heroic mission, more concrete, everyday reason.

What we know about the Japanese concept

In Japanese, ikigai is an everyday word, not a method or a technique. It can refer to something as big as raising a child or as small as morning coffee or tending a vegetable garden. The same person can have several ikigai at once, and they can change with age.

If you want to focus on the word alone —how it’s written, where it comes from and why “reason for being” falls short— I go into it separately in the meaning of the word ikigai.

What ikigai is in Japan

The serious study of ikigai begins with Mieko Kamiya, a Japanese psychiatrist who in 1966 published Ikigai ni tsuite (“On ikigai” or “On the meaning of life”), still the reference text today. Kamiya spent years accompanying patients with serious illnesses and reached an uncomfortable conclusion: lack of meaning could do more harm than the illness itself. Hence her question, which is still the right one: what makes a person feel their life is worth living?

What Kamiya describes —and what anthropologists such as Gordon Matthews would later confirm— is an ikigai far less spectacular than the poster’s. It isn’t a formula combining passion, talent, market and money. It’s an experience: the feeling that there’s something worth living for, and that you have a place and a task. It often lies in small, repeated things, not in a great discovery.

Japanese ikigai is less like a destination and more like a way of inhabiting your days.

There’s also a communal ikigai the West tends to overlook: in Japan, many of the reasons to live are woven with others —family, work done well, the role you play in your surroundings. It isn’t an individual project pursued alone. It’s something held up between people.

The four-circle diagram: where it really came from

If you’ve ever searched for “ikigai”, you’ve seen the diagram: four circles —what you love, what you are good at, what the world needs, what you can be paid for— and, where all four meet, your ikigai. It’s clean, memorable and has practically nothing to do with Japan.

Its history is traceable. In 2011, Spanish author Andrés Zuzunaga published a four-circle diagram about purpose —not ikigai— crossing what you love, what you have a talent for, what the world needs and what you can be paid for. In 2014, British blogger Marc Winn took that same framework, replaced the word “purpose” with “ikigai” —inspired by a Dan Buettner TED talk on longevity in Okinawa— and published it on his blog. That crossover went viral and became the image the world associates with ikigai.

What the West added

The diagram was born talking about purpose and was labelled ikigai afterwards. On top of that came, above all since the international success of Héctor García and Francesc Miralles’ book (2016), the association between ikigai, longevity and happiness. These are Western ideas about a Japanese term, not the Japanese term itself.

Not being Japanese doesn’t make it useless. The diagram works well as an exercise to clear your head when you’re lost in your work or vocation. The problem isn’t the diagram: it’s selling it as ancestral wisdom and, above all, suggesting you only have ikigai if you manage to fit all four things together —including being paid for it. Japanese ikigai demands none of that. An ikigai may not earn money, the world may never notice, and it’s still an ikigai.

Ikigai and purpose: similar, not the same

Since the diagram was born talking about purpose, it’s easy to think ikigai is simply the Japanese word for “life purpose”. They’re alike, but not the same.

  • Purpose, as we usually understand it in the West, points to a grand direction: a mission, a goal that organises everything else. It tends to be singular and to look to the future.
  • Ikigai is more everyday and more plural. It’s the reasons —often small— why it’s worth being here today. You can have many, and they live mostly in the present.

The difference matters because it changes what you look for. If you believe you have to find the purpose of your life, it’s easy to freeze, waiting for a revelation. If you understand that you can recognise several reasons to live that are already there, the task stops being finding something huge and becomes paying attention.

Common mistakes when looking for your ikigai

  • Treating it like a test with a single answer. Ikigai isn’t a result calculated by filling in boxes, but something recognised over time.
  • Demanding that it pays. The popular diagram puts “what you’re paid for” into the equation. The original ikigai doesn’t need it.
  • Looking for something big and dismissing the small. Many reasons to live lie in everyday life, precisely where we don’t look.
  • Confusing productivity with meaning. Doing lots of things isn’t the same as them being worthwhile.
  • Copying someone else’s ikigai. It can’t be inherited or imitated; you recognise your own, which is almost never as photogenic as someone else’s.

The IKIGAIER perspective: doing and being

So far, the shared concept. What follows is IKIGAIER’s own perspective, and it’s worth saying clearly so as not to blur the layers: it isn’t Japanese tradition, but a framework developed by Manel Rueda from his own work and books. It springs precisely from the suspicion this page opened with: that the ikigai we were told about falls short.

The popular version describes one dimension well —what you do— and leaves out the other —where you do it from. IKIGAIER distinguishes two:

Active Ikigai

The dimension of doing. What you build, contribute and develop: your work, your projects, what you leave in the world. It’s what the four-circle diagram tries to describe.

Conscious Ikigai

The dimension of being. Where you do what you do from: with what presence, with what coherence, without betraying yourself in the small decisions. It isn’t measured in achievements, but in how you inhabit your life.

The idea is simple, which is why it’s easy to forget: you can build a flawless life on the active side —goals met, solid CV— and at the same time be quite far from yourself. The question isn’t only what you do, but who you’re being while you do it. An ikigaier, in this framework, is someone who learns not to separate the two: who does from being.

You may not know it, but you’ve been walking far from yourself for a while.

How to start discovering your ikigai

Less like someone solving a diagram and more like someone observing their own life honestly. You don’t need to retreat to a mountain or wait for great clarity: it’s enough to look at what’s already happening and ask yourself a few questions without rushing the answer.

Questions to walk with, not to answer all at once

  • What do you do that absorbs you until you lose track of time?
  • What would you keep doing even if nobody saw it or thanked you for it?
  • At what moment of the day, however small, do you feel you’re where you should be?
  • Where have you drifted away from yourself to meet what was expected?
  • What mask are you holding up that you no longer need?

An example, to bring it down to earth: someone who devotes years to a demanding, successful career may discover their ikigai wasn’t in the position, but in teaching —in explaining something and seeing another person understand it. They don’t have to drop everything; sometimes it’s enough to recognise where the reason lies and give it room. Other times it does mean a change. The point is that first you have to see it, and to see it you have to stop.

Frequently asked questions

What does ikigai mean?

Ikigai (生き甲斐) is a Japanese word made up of iki (to live) and gai (worth, what is worthwhile). It is usually translated as “reason to live” or “what makes life worth living”. It doesn’t refer to a great mission, but to what gives meaning to each day.

Is ikigai the four-circle diagram?

No. That diagram isn’t Japanese and doesn’t describe the original ikigai. It grew out of a framework about purpose by author Andrés Zuzunaga (2011) and was linked to the word ikigai in a 2014 post by blogger Marc Winn. It went viral, but it’s a Western construction.

Is ikigai the same as life purpose?

Not exactly. Purpose, as understood in the West, usually points to a goal or a grand direction. Japanese ikigai is more everyday and plural: the reasons, often small, why you get up each morning. You can have several at once.

How do you find your ikigai?

Less by filling in a diagram and more by observing your life honestly: what absorbs you, what you’d do even if nobody saw, where you’ve drifted away from yourself. You don’t find it by thinking it through once, but by paying attention to what already holds you up.
